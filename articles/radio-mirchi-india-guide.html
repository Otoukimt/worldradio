Radio Mirchi 98.3: inside India's biggest FM network

Updated November 2026 · 7 min read

Mirchi means chilli. The tagline promises that Mirchi listeners are always happy. Neither detail sounds like the branding of one of the largest radio networks on earth, and that informality is exactly why it works. Radio Mirchi began in Indore in 1993, expanded aggressively through India's licence auctions, and now broadcasts on 98.3 FM across dozens of Indian cities with a reach that few Western stations come close to.

What Indian commercial radio is not allowed to do

Here is the detail that explains almost everything about the station's sound. Private FM stations in India have historically been barred from broadcasting their own news. The result was not a weaker medium but a different one: without news to fill hours, private radio poured everything into music, film songs, comedy, phone-ins and the personality of the presenter. The Indian radio jockey became a celebrity in a way the Western DJ largely stopped being.

The music

Bollywood playback songs dominate, and that is not a limitation. Indian film music is one of the largest recorded music industries in the world, with a catalogue stretching back to the 1940s and a production rate no Western chart matches. Mirchi moves between current film releases, nineties nostalgia blocks and regional-language programming depending on the city you are listening to — the Mumbai feed and the Chennai feed are genuinely different stations.

What a non-Hindi speaker gets out of it

  • A catalogue you cannot reach any other way. Most of this music is not on international playlists and will never be recommended to you by an algorithm trained on Western listening.
  • Melody-led arrangements. Film songs are written to carry a scene, so they tend to be structurally richer than a three-minute pop single.
  • Excellent background listening. Lyrics in a language you do not read do not compete for your attention, which is the principle behind our focus listening guide.

Timing matters here

India is on UTC+5:30, so the Mumbai morning show lands in the middle of the European night. For a listener in North Africa or Europe, the useful window is early morning your time — which catches the Indian evening drive slot, the station's liveliest hours.

A station that was forbidden from broadcasting news ended up inventing a form of radio built entirely on personality. Constraints do that.
