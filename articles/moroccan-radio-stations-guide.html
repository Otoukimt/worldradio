Moroccan radio stations: a complete listening guide

Updated 2026-10-31

Moroccan radio is one of the most listened-to media in the country and one of the least documented in English. The dial carries a mix you will not find anywhere else: Arabic and French in the same sentence, Amazigh programming with deep regional roots, Quran recitation around the clock, football commentary that stops cafés mid-conversation, and a youth pop scene that helped launch most of the country's rappers. This guide explains who broadcasts what, why the landscape looks the way it does, and how to listen from anywhere.

How Moroccan radio got here

For most of the twentieth century, broadcasting in Morocco meant the state. Radio-Maroc became Radiodiffusion-Télévision Marocaine (RTM), and the airwaves stayed a public monopoly. Two things changed that.

The first was institutional. The High Authority for Audiovisual Communication (HACA) was created in 2002 by royal decree to regulate the sector, and the 2005 Audiovisual Communication Law formally ended the state's monopoly over broadcasting. In the same year RTM was restructured into the SNRT, the national radio and television company.

The second was commercial. In May 2006, HACA issued its first wave of private radio licences, followed by a second wave in 2009. Almost every private station Moroccans listen to today dates from that opening — which is why the country's radio scene feels young even though radio itself is not.

Two stations sit outside that story. Radio 2M, part of the public-private SOREAD group, was licensed earlier. And Medi1 predates the whole framework: founded in 1980 in Tangier, it was for decades the most distinctive voice on the Moroccan dial. In 2024 the SNRT acquired a majority stake in Medi1 Radio, which moved the country's best-known private station into the public fold.

The public stations: SNRT

The SNRT runs several national radio channels alongside a network of regional stations covering cities from Tangier and Oujda to Agadir, Laâyoune and Dakhla. Four national channels matter most to a listener abroad.

Al Idaa Al Watania (SNRT Radio National)

The flagship Arabic channel, broadcast from Rabat. News, talk, sport and popular music, with a deliberate emphasis on Moroccan productions and a large archive of older recordings that still get regular airtime. It also carries live sports coverage at weekends. If you want to hear what mainstream Morocco sounds like rather than what exports well, start here.

Chaîne Inter

The multilingual channel, built around French with other languages in the schedule. Its tone is closer to European public radio than to the commercial stations, and it is the easiest entry point for a listener who speaks French but no Arabic.

Radio Amazighe

One of the most significant channels on the dial and the one least known outside Morocco. It broadcasts in Tamazight, Tachelhit and Tarifit — the three main Amazigh varieties — carrying music, oral poetry and programming rooted in the Rif, the Middle Atlas and the Souss. For anyone interested in North African music beyond what reaches international playlists, this station is the single best source.

Radio Mohammed VI of the Holy Quran (Assadissa)

Continuous Quran recitation and religious programming. It is among the most consistently streamed Moroccan channels, and its audience extends well beyond the country — particularly during Ramadan, when listening rises sharply across the Maghreb and the diaspora.

The private stations, by what they actually do

Founded in 1980 and broadcast from Tangier, Medi1 was built as a Maghreb-wide station rather than a Moroccan one, and it still sounds like it: generalist, bilingual Arabic and French, strong on news, economics and society. It long reached far beyond FM through powerful long-wave transmission from Nador. It remains the reference station for serious listening in Morocco.

What to listen to, and when

The quickest way to understand a country is to listen to what it plays at seven in the morning, and what it argues about at ten at night.

Listening from outside Morocco

Nearly every station above streams online, and none of them are geo-blocked — Moroccan broadcasters generally want the diaspora audience, which is large in France, Spain, Belgium, Italy and the Netherlands. Three practical notes:

  • Some Moroccan stations still publish insecure (http) stream links, which browsers block on secure sites. If a station refuses to start, that is usually why, and only the broadcaster can fix it.
  • Morocco is on UTC+1. Morning shows run roughly 07:00–10:00 and the main evening talk slots start after 21:00 — worth knowing if you want live programming rather than whatever is playing.
  • During Ramadan the schedules change completely across every station, with the heaviest listening between the two meals. It is the single most interesting month to explore the Moroccan dial.

A note on language

Moroccan radio moves between Darija, Modern Standard Arabic, French and Amazigh — sometimes within a single sentence. Newcomers often assume a station is broken or badly produced when a presenter switches language mid-thought. It is not: code-switching is simply how Moroccans speak, and radio reflects it more honestly than any other medium.

Why Moroccan radio is worth your time

Streaming platforms have flattened music discovery into a global sameness; the same forty songs circulate everywhere. Moroccan radio has not been flattened, partly because much of what it plays was never uploaded in a form an algorithm could find. Chaabi recordings, Amazigh ahwach, Gnawa sessions, Rai crossing in from Oran, and a rap scene writing in Darija about things no playlist curator in London is tracking — all of it is on the air right now, free, in real time.
